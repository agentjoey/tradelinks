## Who this is for

This guide is for cross-border e-commerce sellers — manufacturers, brand owners, and resellers based outside the United States — who sell, or plan to sell, pet supplies into the US market through Amazon US or their own Shopify storefront. It covers the main product families: pet food and treats, pet supplements, and pet toys and accessories such as collars, leashes, beds, bowls, and grooming tools.

It applies when you are the party placing the product on the US market, regardless of fulfillment method. It is an orientation document: which regulatory regimes exist, which concepts drive classification, and where the load-bearing details live. It does not replace review of the primary sources or advice from a qualified US regulatory adviser.

## What changes the decision

The single most important variable in this category is what the product is and what you say about it. The following factors move a seller between regimes:

- Product composition and intended use. A food or treat — something consumed for nutrition, taste, or as a chew — falls under the animal food framework. A non-consumable good, such as a toy, leash, or bed, falls under general consumer product rules. The same item can shift category depending on how it is marketed.
- Claims made on the label, listing, and marketing. A product marketed to treat, cure, mitigate, or prevent disease in animals is regulated as a drug under US law, regardless of its ingredients. Claims language, not seller intent, drives this outcome. This is the classification trap most cross-border sellers fall into.
- Whether the product is ingestible. Anything an animal eats or is dosed with carries food or drug obligations, including ingredient, labeling, and facility-related requirements. Non-ingestible accessories do not.
- Battery or wireless content. Electronic pet products — GPS trackers, automatic feeders, radio bark collars — add equipment-authorization considerations outside this guide's scope.
- Intended user. Products marketed for use by or around children can attract additional consumer product safety obligations.
- Fulfillment path. On Amazon the platform acts as a gatekeeper and may request documentation. On your own Shopify store no gatekeeper exists and all duties land directly on you.
- Destination states. Several states impose requirements beyond the federal baseline, particularly on labeling registration and chemical warnings. California is the most prominent example.

## US requirements

Pet food and treats: animal food under the FD&C Act. Pet food and treats are regulated in the United States as animal food under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act). The responsible agency is the US Food and Drug Administration, acting through its Center for Veterinary Medicine (CVM). The codified regulations appear in Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations (21 CFR), which contains the animal food provisions, including current good manufacturing practice requirements established under the FDA Food Safety Modernization Act (FSMA). Under this framework, pet food must be safe, produced under sanitary conditions, and truthfully labeled, and its ingredients must be acceptable for use in animal food; ingredients that are neither approved food additives nor generally recognized as safe for their intended use can render the product adulterated.

The labeling baseline: AAFCO model regulations. Day-to-day pet food labeling in the US is shaped by the model regulations published by the Association of American Feed Control Officials (AAFCO), a voluntary association of state and federal feed control officials. Most US states have adopted some version of the AAFCO model regulations into state law, which makes AAFCO the practical national labeling baseline even though AAFCO itself is not a government regulator. The AAFCO framework covers product naming rules, guaranteed analysis, ingredient statements in descending order of predominance by weight, nutritional adequacy statements, feeding directions, and the net quantity statement. Many states also require product-by-product registration or licensing of pet food before sale; the specific fees and renewal cycles vary by state and are `[UNVERIFIED — confirm at source]`, to be checked state by state. Cross-border sellers should treat AAFCO conformity as the labeling floor and then check the registration rules of the states they ship into.

Claims discipline: the drug line. This is the highest-risk concept in the category. Under the FD&C Act, a product intended to cure, treat, mitigate, or prevent disease in animals is a drug. FDA regulates such products under its new animal drug authorities in 21 CFR, and marketing a new animal drug without the required approval is unlawful. The practical consequence: a treat or supplement whose label, listing copy, or advertising claims that it treats arthritis, cures anxiety as a medical condition, or prevents disease has crossed from food into drug territory, however benign the ingredients. The concept to hold onto is intended use as established by claims. Structure/function-style language — supporting normal joint function or maintaining healthy skin rather than treating a named disease — occupies a narrower space, and the boundary is fact-specific. Specific examples of permitted and prohibited claim wording are `[UNVERIFIED — confirm at source]`; validate exact claims language against FDA guidance and CVM enforcement history before listing.

Animal supplements: the gray zone. Products marketed as dietary supplements for animals have no dedicated federal supplement category comparable to the one for human dietary supplements. FDA has historically exercised enforcement discretion toward many animal supplement products while maintaining that they are, depending on claims and composition, either foods or unapproved drugs. Industry practice has developed around this gray zone, and some trade associations maintain their own quality and labeling programs, but none of that changes the underlying legal analysis: claims that cross into disease treatment or prevention create drug exposure. This guide does not advise whether any particular supplement product is lawful; the space is unsettled, enforcement is complaint- and risk-driven, and sellers bear the classification risk themselves.

Pet toys and accessories: no dedicated federal pet-toy regime. There is no US federal statute dedicated specifically to pet toys or pet accessories. Non-food pet products are general consumer products, and the applicable federal framework is the general jurisdiction of the US Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) together with the Federal Hazardous Substances Act (FHSA), which addresses hazardous substances in consumer products and drives concepts such as cautionary labeling and bans on certain dangerous articles. Pet products marketed for use by or with children can fall within CPSC's children's product rules. Where no specific mandatory standard exists, sellers still carry the general duty not to place hazardous products on the market, and CPSC can and does pursue recalls of pet products that injure pets or people — including leashes, toys, and containment products. Practical obligations include hazard assessment of the design (choking-size parts, strangulation risks, toxic coatings), accurate labeling, and reporting substantial product hazards to CPSC when a seller becomes aware of them. Recall exposure is real in this category and is typically driven by injury reports.

State layer: California Proposition 65 and beyond. Beyond the federal instruments, states impose their own requirements. The most commonly encountered for cross-border sellers is California's Proposition 65, which requires warnings before exposure to listed chemicals known to the state to cause cancer or reproductive harm. Proposition 65 applies to pet products sold into California, and private enforcement actions in this space are common. Whether a given product requires a warning depends on its materials and exposure assessment; specific listed chemicals and safe-harbor thresholds are `[UNVERIFIED — confirm at source]`. Other states maintain their own pet food registration regimes and, increasingly, chemical-disclosure or restriction laws affecting consumer products generally. A full fifty-state survey is outside the scope of this guide.

## Amazon US

Amazon acts as a gatekeeper for the pet supplies category on its US marketplace. In general, verifiable terms, sellers should expect the following:

- Documentation requests. Amazon routinely requests compliance documentation for pet products, particularly ingestibles — labeling images, ingredient information, and evidence relating to safety or registration. Requests can arrive before or after a listing goes live, sometimes triggered by customer complaints or automated review. Sellers who cannot produce documentation promptly risk listing removal.
- Category and product gating. Some pet subcategories require approval before listing. Gating criteria change over time and are published in Seller Central; current requirements for any product type are `[UNVERIFIED — confirm at source]` and must be checked in the seller's own account.
- Claims enforcement. Amazon enforces its own restricted-products and claims policies in addition to the legal baseline. Listings making disease-treatment claims for pet supplements or treats are a known enforcement target, and removal does not require an FDA action first.
- Recall and safety action. When a product is subject to a recall or safety alert, Amazon may suppress listings, notify past buyers, and withhold inventory. Sellers remain responsible for recall execution even when Amazon facilitates customer notification.

None of this substitutes for the legal duties described above; Amazon's processes sit on top of them.

## Shopify US

Running your own Shopify storefront changes the compliance posture but not the underlying law. Shopify is a storefront and payments infrastructure provider, not a marketplace gatekeeper of the kind Amazon is. There is no category gating, no proactive documentation request before you list, and no platform compliance team reviewing your claims in advance.

The consequence is that every duty described in this guide lands directly on you: pet food labeling conformity, the food-versus-drug claims line, CPSC reporting duties, state registration requirements, and Proposition 65 warning obligations. Shopify's acceptable-use and legal terms require you to comply with applicable law, and Shopify can suspend stores in response to regulatory action, rights-holder complaints, or payment-processor pressure, but it does not verify your regulatory position for you.

Sellers who rely on Amazon's gating as their de facto compliance check should not assume that a product that passed Amazon review is therefore compliant for direct sale, or the reverse. The two channels demand the same underlying legal position; they differ only in who checks and when.
