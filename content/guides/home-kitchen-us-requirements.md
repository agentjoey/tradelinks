## Who this is for

This guide is for cross-border e-commerce sellers who list home and kitchen products into the United States, whether through Amazon US or through their own Shopify storefront. It applies when you manufacture, import, or resell physical goods such as cookware, food-storage containers, tableware, ceramic and glass drinkware, small electrical kitchen appliances, candles, household chemical products, and textile kitchen goods such as towels, aprons, and oven mitts.

It is orientation, not a compliance determination. It maps the main federal instruments you are likely to encounter, explains what moves a product in or out of scope, and describes how the two main sales channels differ in practice. It does not cover state and local rules, which can be significant in this category, and it does not replace advice from a qualified adviser on your specific product.

## What changes the decision

Whether a requirement applies to you turns on a small set of product facts. Work through them before listing.

- Product composition. The single most important fact is what the product is made of and whether any part of it touches food. Plastics, coatings, adhesives, paper, silicone, and metal surfaces that contact food are regulated differently from purely decorative items. A cutting board, a storage container, and a wall clock sit in different regulatory worlds even if they are sold in the same category browse node.
- Intended and reasonably foreseeable use. A decorative ceramic plate sold for display is treated differently from one sold for serving food. How you describe the product, and how a reasonable consumer would use it, both matter.
- Claims made. Claims such as "microwave safe", "for hot food", "non-toxic", or "BPA-free" can pull a product into food-contact analysis or create substantiation duties. Claims should match what your materials and testing actually support.
- Electrical content. Any mains-powered appliance, and any product with a heating element, motor, or rechargeable battery, raises electrical safety and marketplace certification expectations that do not apply to passive goods.
- Chemical content. Candles, aerosols, cleaning or treatment products, and similar chemical household goods carry federal labeling duties that passive housewares do not.
- Textile content. Fabric items such as towels, aprons, pot holders, and table linens carry fiber-content labeling duties.
- Children's use. Products designed or marketed primarily for children twelve and under enter a separate, stricter regime under the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008. That regime is not covered in depth here; if any part of your range is a children's product, treat that as a separate workstream.
- Fulfillment path. Whether you import as the importer of record, use a fulfillment service, or dropship affects who holds customs obligations, including country-of-origin marking. The obligations do not disappear because a marketplace warehouses the goods.

## US requirements

Food-contact materials. The Food and Drug Administration regulates substances that migrate into food from packaging, cookware, storage, and preparation surfaces as food-contact substances. The core regulations sit in 21 CFR Parts 174 through 178, which cover indirect food additives generally, adjuvants and production aids, and specific material classes such as coatings, polymers, and paper. Two concepts matter most for a seller. First, some substances are "generally recognized as safe" (GRAS) for their intended use, and some are covered by existing regulations or prior sanctions; these do not need new premarket clearance. Second, new food-contact substances that are not already authorized generally reach the market through the Food Contact Notification program, under which a notifier submits data to FDA and the notification becomes effective for that notifier and use after a review period. In practice, most sellers do not file notifications themselves; they buy materials and components from suppliers who hold the relevant clearances, and they keep supplier declarations on file. Specific migration limits, test conditions, and the list of cleared substances for any given material are `[UNVERIFIED — confirm at source]`; confirm against 21 CFR Parts 174-178 and FDA's food-contact inventory for your exact material.

Lead and cadmium in ceramicware and glassware. FDA has long-standing guidance levels for lead leaching from ceramicware used for food, and has issued guidance addressing lead and cadmium in other food-contact articles such as glassware and silver-plated holloware. The specific action levels, and which categories of ware they apply to, are `[UNVERIFIED — confirm at source]`. The practical point is that decorative ceramics not intended for food use are expected to be clearly marked as such, and food-use ceramicware should be supported by leach testing from a competent laboratory against current FDA guidance.

Electrical kitchen appliances. There is no general federal mandate that household electrical appliances be certified before sale. The federal layer for most ordinary appliances is the Consumer Product Safety Commission's authority over product safety and recalls, plus radio-frequency rules for anything with wireless or digital electronics. In practice, however, the US market runs on certification by a Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratory, the NRTL program administered under OSHA: marks such as UL and ETL indicate testing to consensus safety standards. Insurers, retailers, and marketplaces commonly expect an NRTL mark on powered kitchen appliances, and Amazon asks for test evidence in gated subcategories. Treat NRTL certification as a commercial requirement with regulatory weight behind it, not as a federal premarket approval. If the product contains a radio module, separate equipment authorization rules apply; that is outside this guide's scope.

Candles, aerosols, and chemical household products. The Federal Hazardous Substances Act, administered by the CPSC, requires cautionary labeling on hazardous household substances: signal words, hazard statements, precautionary and first-aid information, and the manufacturer's identity. Aerosols, products under pressure, flammable or combustible formulations, and corrosive or toxic products fall within these concepts. Candles sit at the boundary: a plain candle is largely a fire-safety product, while scented or treated candles can attract FHSA labeling analysis. Exact trigger definitions and wording requirements are `[UNVERIFIED — confirm at source]`; review the FHSA and CPSC's implementing regulations for your formulation.

Textile kitchen goods. Textile fiber products sold in the US are subject to the fiber-content labeling rules at 16 CFR Part 303, issued under the Textile Fiber Products Identification Act. In concept, items such as kitchen towels, aprons, and table linens must disclose generic fiber names and percentages, and carry manufacturer identity and country of origin. Whether a specific item, such as an oven mitt with a coated grip, is fully covered, partially exempt, or out of scope depends on its construction; confirm at the regulation text.

Country-of-origin marking. Under 19 U.S.C. 1304, the marking of imported articles statute, every article of foreign origin imported into the United States must be marked in a conspicuous place, legibly, indelibly, and permanently, with the English name of the country of origin, unless an exception applies. This is a customs obligation enforced by US Customs and Border Protection and it attaches to the article and, in many cases, its retail packaging. Sellers who never touch the goods physically still need their suppliers to mark correctly, because unmarked or mismarked goods can be detained, refused, or marked at the importer's expense at the border.

Recalls and continuing duties. Federal compliance does not end at the border. The CPSC expects firms to report products that create a substantial product hazard and conducts recalls across consumer categories, including kitchen goods. A cross-border seller is within reach of these duties when selling into the US; having a way to receive safety complaints and act on them is part of operating in this market.

## Amazon US

Amazon acts as a private gatekeeper layered on top of the federal baseline. In this category, the recurring patterns are documentation requests and gating. Amazon may ask sellers of food-contact products for declarations or test reports supporting material safety; sellers of powered appliances are commonly asked for NRTL test reports naming the applicable standard; and sellers of chemical products may be asked for labeling images and safety data. Amazon's seller-facing compliance pages describe these requests in general terms, and the exact evidence demanded for a given subcategory or listing is set by Amazon and can change. Listings that cannot produce requested documentation are typically suppressed until it is provided. Amazon also operates recall processes and will remove or suppress listings implicated in a CPSC recall. None of this replaces the underlying federal duties; it is additional, contractual, and enforced at the listing level.

## Shopify US

On your own Shopify storefront there is no platform gatekeeper screening your listings. The same federal instruments apply, but the duty lands directly on you: correct FHSA labeling, fiber-content labels, origin marking through your import process, and defensible food-contact sourcing. Shopify's terms require merchants to comply with applicable law, and Shopify can suspend stores for illegal goods, but it does not pre-approve products. In exchange for that freedom you carry the full burden: payment processors and insurers may impose their own evidence expectations, and there is no marketplace compliance team to tell you a document is missing. The channel also changes nothing about recalls and reporting duties once products are in US consumers' hands.
