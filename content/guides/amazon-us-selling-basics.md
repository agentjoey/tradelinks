## Who this is for

This guide is for sellers based outside the United States — or newly entering the US market — who sell, or plan to sell, physical consumer products to US customers through Amazon.com. It applies to the general run of consumer goods rather than to any single regulated category, and it covers the platform and federal fundamentals that recur across nearly every product line: account setup and identity verification, how listings and offers work, why some categories are gated, the choice between Fulfillment by Amazon (FBA) and merchant fulfillment, and the account-health mechanics through which Amazon enforces its rules.

It is orientation, not a checklist for any specific product. If your product falls into a heavily regulated class — food, drugs, medical devices, cosmetics, children's products, electronics with radio transmitters, pesticides — this guide tells you where the federal layer sits, but you must go to the cited instruments and agencies for the operative detail.

## What changes the decision

Whether a given requirement applies to you turns on the product, the claims, and the fulfillment path — not on the fact that you sell through Amazon. The main factors:

- Product composition and function. A product containing a button cell or coin battery falls under 16 CFR Part 1263. A product that emits radio frequency energy — anything with Bluetooth, Wi‑Fi, or an intentional radiator, and many unintentional radiators such as digital devices — falls under FCC equipment authorization rules in 47 CFR Part 15. A cosmetic falls under FDA labeling rules, including 21 CFR Part 701, and the Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act of 2022 (MoCRA) adds facility registration and product listing duties.
- Intended users. A product intended for children twelve years of age or younger is a "children's product" under the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008 (CPSIA), which triggers third-party testing by a CPSC-accepted laboratory and a Children's Product Certificate. General-use products carry a lighter but real certification duty (a General Certificate of Conformity where a CPSC rule applies).
- Claims made. Claims can reclassify a product. A cream marketed to moisturize is a cosmetic; the same cream marketed to treat eczema may be a drug. Health, safety, and origin claims all attract specific federal rules.
- Country of origin. Imported articles generally must be marked with their country of origin under 19 U.S.C. 1304, administered by US Customs and Border Protection.
- Fulfillment path. Using FBA does not transfer compliance duties to Amazon. You remain the seller of record and, for imports, typically the importer of record unless you arrange otherwise. The fulfillment choice changes logistics, returns handling, and fee structure — not the underlying legal duties.
- Business form and tax presence. Identity verification, tax information collection, and payout mechanics depend on where your business is organized and where you have tax nexus. Those details are outside the scope of this guide and should be confirmed with a qualified adviser.

## US requirements

The federal layer applies to the product and the transaction regardless of platform. The instruments below are the ones most commonly load-bearing for cross-border sellers of general consumer goods.

Product safety. The Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008 (CPSIA), administered by the US Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC), requires that consumer products subject to a CPSC safety rule be tested for compliance and certified. For children's products, testing must be performed by a CPSC-accepted third-party laboratory and documented in a Children's Product Certificate; for non-children's products subject to a rule, the manufacturer or importer issues a General Certificate of Conformity. Certificates must accompany the product or shipment and be furnished to distributors and retailers — and, in practice, Amazon routinely requests them. Products containing button cell or coin batteries are subject to 16 CFR Part 1263, a federal safety standard addressing battery compartment security and labeling, enacted under the statute commonly known as Reese's Law.

Radio frequency and electronics. The Federal Communications Commission regulates radio frequency devices under 47 CFR Part 15. Devices that intentionally emit radio energy (Bluetooth, Wi‑Fi, cellular, remote controls) generally require equipment authorization before marketing; many unintentional radiators (digital devices, power supplies, LED drivers) are subject to a Supplier's Declaration of Conformity or certification, depending on the device type. Selling a wireless product in the US without the applicable authorization is a federal issue, not merely a listing issue.

Cosmetics and personal care. Cosmetics are regulated by the US Food and Drug Administration. Labeling requirements appear at 21 CFR Part 701 (ingredient declaration, identity, net quantity, responsible party). MoCRA added facility registration, product listing, adverse event recordkeeping, and safety substantiation duties for cosmetics. Claims that go beyond cosmetic purposes can move the product into drug regulation.

Labeling and origin. Under 19 U.S.C. 1304, every article of foreign origin imported into the United States must be marked with the English name of its country of origin, unless an exception applies. CBP administers this at import; mismarked goods can be detained or refused. Beyond origin marking, specific product classes carry their own labeling statutes and FTC-administered rules (textiles, wool, fur, and care labeling among them); which ones apply depends on the product.

Truthful marketing. The Federal Trade Commission Act prohibits unfair or deceptive acts or practices, which covers unsubstantiated product claims, deceptive reviews and endorsements, and misleading origin claims. The FTC's Endorsement Guides address incentivized and fabricated reviews — a practice Amazon also polices under its own policies.

Customs and admissibility. Importing into the US involves classification, valuation, duty payment, and admissibility screening by CBP, plus partner-agency requirements (CPSC, FDA, FCC, USDA, and others depending on the product). Amazon's fulfillment centers do not act as importer of record; sellers shipping inventory to FBA from abroad must arrange the import in their own name or through a designated importer. Duty rates, de minimis treatment, and tariff classifications are product- and time-specific and are marked here as [UNVERIFIED — confirm at source] rather than stated generally.

State and local rules. State-level obligations — including state product-safety statutes, packaging and extended-producer-responsibility laws, and sales tax nexus rules — exist alongside the federal layer. They are out of scope for this guide.

## Amazon US

Amazon's relationship to these duties is set out in the Amazon Services Business Solutions Agreement, the contract every seller accepts at registration. Two of its recurring themes matter here: sellers are responsible for complying with all applicable laws, and Amazon may request documentation, suspend listings, or withhold or dispose of inventory when it believes a product or account violates law or policy.

Account and identity verification. Registering a selling account requires identity and business verification — government-issued identification, business registration details, bank account and tax information. Amazon re-verifies periodically and on triggers such as account changes. The exact document set varies by seller location and account type; treat Amazon's own registration flow as the source of truth.

Listings versus offers. Amazon's catalog separates the product detail page (identified by an ASIN) from individual offers against that page. Multiple sellers can attach offers to one detail page; creating a new detail page is appropriate only when no matching page exists. Contributing inaccurate content to a shared page — or creating duplicate pages to escape reviews or enforcement history — is a policy violation, because the page is shared infrastructure, not the seller's property.

Category and product gating. Some categories and products require approval before listing. Approval requests commonly ask for invoices, test reports, or certificates — for children's products, the Children's Product Certificate and associated test reports are the standard example; for other categories, safety data sheets, compliance certificates, or brand authorization may be requested. Which categories are gated and what documents are required changes over time and is published in Seller Central; this guide deliberately names no ASIN-level rules. When Amazon requests documentation, the underlying federal instrument (CPSIA certification, FCC authorization, FDA registration) is what the document must substantiate — Amazon's request does not replace the duty.

Fulfillment choice. Under FBA, Amazon stores, picks, packs, and ships, and handles much of customer service and returns; under merchant fulfillment, the seller does. FBA carries storage and fulfillment fees and inventory-performance consequences; merchant fulfillment carries direct control and direct exposure to shipping-performance metrics. Both paths leave product compliance, import duties, and recall responsibility with the seller. Fee schedules change and are published by Amazon; specific amounts are not restated here.

Account health and enforcement. Amazon tracks policy compliance and performance through an account health dashboard, covering policy violations (suspected intellectual property complaints, product condition and safety complaints, restricted products) and performance metrics (order defect rate, cancellation rate, late shipment rate). Enforcement escalates in general terms from listing removal, through policy warnings, to account deactivation; sellers typically receive an opportunity to respond or appeal, and serious or repeated violations can forfeit that opportunity. Safety incidents can also trigger Amazon-led recall processes in which sellers are expected to cooperate.

Fees and payments. Referral fees, fulfillment fees, and storage fees apply per the published schedules; disbursements follow a settlement cycle and can be reserved or withheld during investigations. ## Shopify US

Running your own storefront on Shopify removes the platform gatekeeper but not a single federal duty. There is no category gating and no account-health dashboard; instead, every obligation described above — CPSC certification, FCC authorization, FDA labeling, CBP origin marking, FTC advertising rules, recall conduct — lands directly on you, and enforcement comes from agencies, customs holds, payment-processor risk reviews, and private litigation rather than from a platform warning. Shopify's own terms require lawful conduct and permit Shopify to suspend stores, and payment processors apply their own compliance screening, but no one reviews your documentation before you sell. Practically, the self-hosted seller must build the compliance file proactively: test reports, certificates, labeling review, and import records, kept current without a platform prompt.
