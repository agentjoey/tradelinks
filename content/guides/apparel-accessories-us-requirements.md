## Who this is for

This guide is for sellers based outside the United States — and US-based sellers importing finished goods — who sell apparel or textile accessories (scarves, hats, gloves, belts with textile content) into the US market through Amazon.com or their own Shopify storefront. It applies when the product is a textile product in the ordinary sense: garments and fabric-based accessories intended for general use.

It applies whether you hold inventory in the US, fulfill cross-border, or use a fulfillment network: US federal labeling and safety rules attach to the product when it enters US commerce, and the importer of record and the seller are the parties regulators and platforms look to. If your products are entirely non-textile, most of this guide does not apply; if any portion is a children's product, the children's rules layer on top of everything else here.

## What changes the decision

The factors that move you between rule sets:

- Fiber composition. Whether the product is all-cotton, a blend, wool, fur-trimmed, or leather changes which labeling statute applies and what the label must say.
- Children's use. A product designed or intended primarily for children 12 years of age or younger is a "children's product" under the CPSIA and attracts third-party testing, certification, and tracking-label duties that adult apparel does not.
- Sleepwear versus daywear. Children's sleepwear and certain sleepwear-adjacent garments fall under dedicated flammability standards that ordinary apparel does not.
- Claims on the listing or label. Terms like "organic," "bamboo," "hypoallergenic," "flame resistant," or a made-in claim each carry substantiation duties and, in some cases, dedicated FTC rules.
- Country of origin. Origin determines the marking required on the product and its customs treatment; for textiles it is also a mandatory label element.
- Fulfillment path. Importing a commercial shipment makes you the importer of record with declaration duties; shipping individual parcels direct to consumers does not remove the labeling and safety duties, even if enforcement attention differs.
- Platform. Amazon adds a documentation-and-gating layer on top of the law. Shopify does not; the duties land on you directly.

## US requirements

### FTC textile labeling

Two instruments govern how most apparel must be labeled in the United States.

The Textile Fiber Products Identification Act, implemented by the FTC at 16 CFR Part 303, requires most textile wearing apparel and certain other textile products to carry a label disclosing three things: the generic fiber names and percentages by weight of the fibers present, the country of origin, and the identity of the manufacturer or another responsible business — typically its name or an FTC-issued registered identification number (an RN). Placement, conspicuousness, and attachment rules are set out in Part 303 itself. Blends must list fibers in descending order of predominance, subject to tolerance rules in the regulation. Advertising rules under the same part apply fiber and origin disclosures to online product descriptions — directly relevant to a cross-border e-commerce listing.

The Care Labeling Rule, 16 CFR Part 423, requires manufacturers and importers of most apparel to attach a permanent label giving regular care instructions — washing, drying, ironing, bleaching, or drycleaning directions adequate for the ordinary useful life of the garment. The rule addresses approved care symbol systems under conditions stated in the rule; confirm details against the current text of Part 423, as it has been amended over time.

### Flammability

The Flammable Fabrics Act authorizes flammability standards for apparel, administered by the CPSC. The general wearing apparel standard is the Standard for the Flammability of Clothing Textiles, 16 CFR Part 1610. It classifies fabrics by burn behavior and prohibits fabrics in the dangerously flammable class from use in apparel, with exemptions and testing provisions in the part itself. Importers and sellers are responsible for ensuring the garments they sell meet the standard.

Children's sleepwear is covered by separate, more stringent standards: 16 CFR Part 1615 (sizes 0 through 6X) and 16 CFR Part 1616 (sizes 7 through 14). These apply to children's sleepwear and similar garments in the covered size ranges; the detailed requirements — test methods, flame-resistance criteria, labeling, and the tight-fitting-sleepwear provisions — are not summarized here and are [UNVERIFIED — confirm at source]. Read Parts 1615 and 1616 directly before listing children's sleepwear.

### Children's apparel and CPSIA

The Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008 (CPSIA) imposes duties on all children's products — products designed or intended primarily for children 12 years of age or younger — and children's apparel is included. In general terms:

- Lead. Children's products are subject to limits on lead in accessible substrate materials and lead in paint and surface coatings, under 15 U.S.C. 1278a and the implementing CPSC framework. Textile components, prints, snaps, zippers, and appliqués on children's garments are all in scope conceptually.
- Phthalates. Section 108 of the CPSIA restricts certain phthalates in children's toys and child care articles. Whether soft plasticized components of a garment fall within scope for a given configuration is [UNVERIFIED — confirm at source].
- Third-party testing and certification. Most children's products must be tested by a CPSC-accepted third-party laboratory, and the importer or domestic manufacturer must issue a Children's Product Certificate (CPC) based on that testing, under 15 U.S.C. 2063. The certificate must accompany the product through the supply chain.
- Tracking labels. 15 U.S.C. 2063(a)(5) requires children's products to bear permanent distinguishing marks — a tracking label — allowing the purchaser to identify the manufacturer, production location and date, and cohort information such as batch or run number.

These duties parallel the children's-product obligations described in our toys guide; the concept is the same — testing, a Children's Product Certificate, and a tracking label — applied to garments rather than toys. Cross-reference that guide for the shared CPSIA framework.

### Drawstrings on children's outerwear

Drawstrings at the hood or neck of children's upper outerwear are a recognized strangulation hazard. The CPSC has published guidance on drawstrings on children's upper outerwear, built on the ASTM F1816 voluntary standard. This is agency guidance rather than a mandatory codified standard, but CPSC treats non-conforming drawstrings as presenting a substantial product hazard and has supported recalls on that basis; certain state-level rules and platform policies also reference it. Treat the CPSC drawstring guidance as effectively binding in practice for children's jackets, hoodies, and similar outerwear.

### Wool and fur

Where a product is wool or fur rather than an ordinary textile, separate federal labeling statutes apply: the Wool Products Labeling Act and the Fur Products Labeling Act, each with its own FTC rules. ### Customs and import

Textiles and apparel are among the more heavily administered categories at US entry. In general terms:

- Country-of-origin marking. Imported articles must be marked with their country of origin under 19 U.S.C. 1304; for textile products this obligation aligns with, but is legally distinct from, the FTC origin-label requirement.
- Import declarations and classification. Entry of textile products requires accurate classification under the Harmonized Tariff Schedule and complete entry documentation through US Customs and Border Protection (CBP). Textile entry documentation concepts include declarations covering fiber content, origin, and manufacturing details; the exact forms and data elements are [UNVERIFIED — confirm at source].
- Quota and visa. Historically, certain textile and apparel imports were subject to quota and visa arrangements, and a small number of such arrangements may still exist for specific origins and categories. Whether any quota or visa requirement applies to your product and origin is [UNVERIFIED — confirm at source]; check CBP's textile import guidance before a commercial shipment.
- Duties. Apparel carries some of the higher tariff lines in the US schedule, but rates depend on classification and origin and are not stated here. Any duty figure for a specific SKU is [UNVERIFIED — confirm at source].

If you ship direct-to-consumer parcels, low-value entries may be processed under simplified procedures, but the labeling, flammability, and CPSIA duties above attach to the product regardless of entry method.

## Amazon US

Amazon layers marketplace policy on top of the federal rules. In general terms:

- Amazon's seller policies require listings to comply with applicable law, and textile-category listings are expected to carry accurate fiber-content and origin information consistent with FTC rules; mislabeled fiber content is a recurring enforcement theme.
- Amazon operates product-compliance documentation workflows: for certain categories — children's products prominently among them — it may request test reports or a Children's Product Certificate from a CPSC-accepted laboratory, and may suppress or remove listings that cannot produce them.
- Category gating and brand-approval mechanisms exist across apparel; whether a specific subcategory or brand is gated is determined inside Seller Central and changes over time, so confirm there rather than relying on third-party summaries.
- Amazon treats safety documentation requests as part of its recall and regulatory-response posture; inability to supply documentation can lead to listing removal and, in persistent cases, account action.

None of this replaces the legal duties. Amazon's checks are spot checks; compliance remains the seller's responsibility whether or not Amazon ever asks.

## Shopify US

On a Shopify storefront there is no platform gatekeeper performing compliance review on your listings. The consequence runs the other way: every duty described in the US requirements section lands directly on you — FTC textile and care labeling, flammability compliance, CPSIA testing and certification for children's items, drawstring conformance, and customs obligations as importer.

Operationally this means holding your own compliance file per product: label artwork and physical label specimens, fiber-content and origin substantiation, test reports, Children's Product Certificates where applicable, and customs entry records. Shopify will not ask for these, but the FTC, CPSC, CBP, or a plaintiff's attorney can. Shopify's documentation covers storefront operation; it does not and cannot discharge regulatory duties.

Payment processors, ad platforms, and marketplaces connected to a Shopify store may impose their own product rules; those are contractual layers, not law, and vary.
