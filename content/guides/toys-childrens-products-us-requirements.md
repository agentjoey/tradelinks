## Who this is for

This guide is for cross-border sellers — typically based outside the United States — who manufacture, import, or resell toys or other products intended for children and sell them into the US market through Amazon US or a Shopify storefront.

It applies to you if any of the following is true: your product is designed or marketed primarily for children 12 years of age or younger; your listing copy, images, or packaging suggests children's use; or your product is a toy within the meaning of the US toy safety standard, regardless of how you describe it. It also applies if you are a reseller rather than the manufacturer, because US law assigns specific duties to importers and, in some cases, to domestic sellers of record.

This category is the strictest corner of US consumer product regulation. The penalties for non-compliance are severe, the enforcement agencies are active, and platforms treat missing documentation as a reason to suppress or remove listings without negotiation. If you are not prepared to meet the federal testing and certification requirements described below, the correct commercial decision is not to sell the product in the US. There is no low-effort path through this category.

## What changes the decision

Several factors determine which specific rules attach to your product.

- **Intended user age.** The dividing line in federal law is whether a product is designed or intended primarily for children 12 years of age or younger. That determination looks at the manufacturer's stated intent, marketing, packaging, and whether consumers would commonly recognize the product as being for children. Calling a product "for ages 14+" does not settle the question if the product is obviously a child's item.
- **Toy versus general children's product.** A toy is a children's product and carries additional obligations, including conformity to the toy safety standard. Non-toy children's products — clothing, furniture, feeding items, school supplies — carry the general children's product duties plus any product-specific rules.
- **Chemical composition.** Materials, paints, surface coatings, and plasticized components determine whether lead and phthalate limits are directly implicated. Accessible substrates and paint or similar surface coatings are treated separately under the statute.
- **Small parts.** Products intended for children under three years of age are subject to a small-parts prohibition. Products with small parts intended for older children carry specific choking-hazard warning labels.
- **Batteries.** Products containing button cell or coin batteries are subject to a dedicated federal safety standard with construction, testing, and labeling requirements.
- **Claims and labeling.** Age grading, safety warnings, country-of-origin marking, and tracking labels are all regulated. Claims you make — or omit — on the product and packaging can themselves be violations.
- **Fulfillment path.** Selling through Amazon shifts some evidence production to a platform-driven documentation request process. Selling through your own Shopify store leaves every duty directly with you, with no intermediary screening but also no intermediary protection.

## US requirements

The federal anchor for this category is the **Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008 (CPSIA)**, which amended the Consumer Product Safety Act and gave the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) expanded authority over children's products. Its core obligations, in outline:

**Third-party testing.** Children's products subject to a children's product safety rule must be tested for compliance by a third-party conformity assessment body — a laboratory accepted by the CPSC — before the product is imported or distributed in commerce. Testing by the manufacturer's own lab, or by a lab not on the CPSC's accepted list, does not satisfy the requirement. The CPSC maintains a public list of accepted laboratories.

**Children's Product Certificate (CPC).** Based on the passing test results, the manufacturer or the importer must issue a Children's Product Certificate certifying that the product complies with all applicable children's product safety rules. The certificate must identify the product, cite each applicable rule, and identify the manufacturer or importer, the testing laboratory, and the date and place of manufacture and testing. The CPC must "accompany" the product and be furnished to distributors and retailers; in practice, you must be able to produce it to CPSC, to customs authorities, and to Amazon on request. A CPC based on fabricated, outdated, or non-corresponding test reports is treated as a false certification.

**Tracking labels.** Children's products must bear a tracking label on the product and its packaging, to the extent practicable, allowing the manufacturer and the purchaser to trace the product to its production run — manufacturer, location and date of production, and cohort information such as a batch or run number. This is what makes targeted recalls possible; its absence is itself a violation.

**Lead limits.** CPSIA imposes a limit of 100 parts per million total lead content in accessible component parts (substrates) of children's products, and a separate, lower limit for lead in paint and similar surface coatings. These figures are widely published by CPSC, but the precise applicable limit, exemptions, and effective dates for your specific product must be confirmed against the current text of the statute and the implementing regulations at source before you rely on them.

**Phthalate limits.** CPSIA prohibits children's toys and child care articles containing more than 0.1 percent of specified phthalates; the statute and subsequent CPSC rulemaking cover eight listed phthalates. Again, the figure of 0.1 percent is widely published, but the current list of covered phthalates and the products in scope should be confirmed against the current regulatory text.

**Toy safety standard.** Toys sold in the US must conform to the mandatory toy standard: **16 CFR Part 1250**, which incorporates by reference **ASTM F963**, the Standard Consumer Safety Specification for Toy Safety. ASTM F963 covers mechanical and physical hazards, flammability, and chemical requirements for toys. Your third-party lab tests the toy against this standard, and the CPC cites it.

**Small parts and age labeling.** Products intended for children under three are subject to the small-parts ban in **16 CFR Part 1501**, which uses a standardized test cylinder to define what counts as a small part. Separately, the **Federal Hazardous Substances Act (FHSA)** requires cautionary labeling for hazardous substances and for toys and games containing small parts, balloons, small balls, or marbles when intended for older children — the familiar choking-hazard warnings are FHSA labeling. Age grading on your packaging must be consistent with these rules, not just with your marketing preference.

**Button and coin batteries.** Children's products — and consumer products generally — containing button cell or coin batteries are subject to **16 CFR Part 1263**, which implements Reese's Law. It imposes performance requirements for battery compartments (so a child cannot readily access the battery), testing, and specific warning labels on packaging and instructions. If your product contains a coin cell, this rule is not optional and applies in addition to everything above.

**Recalls and reporting.** Manufacturers, importers, distributors, and retailers have a statutory duty to report to CPSC when they obtain information that a product fails to comply with an applicable rule, contains a defect that could create a substantial product hazard, or creates an unreasonable risk of serious injury or death. Failing to report is itself a violation and has historically produced some of the largest civil penalties in this space. Cross-border sellers are not exempt: the importer of record carries these duties for goods entering the US.

## Amazon US

Amazon treats toys and children's products as a category requiring documentation. In general, verifiable terms:

- Amazon may require sellers of toys and children's products to submit compliance documentation — typically the Children's Product Certificate and the underlying test reports from a CPSC-accepted laboratory — before listing, or at any time after listing, through its compliance documentation request process.
- Listings that cannot be supported with the requested documents within the stated window are subject to suppression or removal. Repeated or serious failures can lead to account-level enforcement.
- Amazon's requirements operate on top of, not instead of, federal law. Having documents accepted by Amazon does not certify legal compliance, and Amazon's request does not limit CPSC's authority to act against the same product.
- Amazon's seller-facing help pages describe which document types it accepts and how submissions are reviewed; the specific document checklist for your product should be read directly from Seller Central at the time you list, because Amazon revises its requirements.

Do not assume that because a listing is live, the product is cleared. Documentation requests commonly arrive after a product has been selling for some time, often triggered by category-wide sweeps or complaints.

## Shopify US

On your own Shopify storefront there is no platform gatekeeper requesting your CPC. That changes the mechanics, not the law.

Every federal duty described above — third-party testing, certification, tracking labels, lead and phthalate limits, ASTM F963 conformity, small-parts rules, FHSA labeling, battery requirements, reporting duties — applies in full when you sell directly. The difference is that no one checks your paperwork before the first sale. The check arrives later, in one of several forms: a CPSC inquiry or investigation, a customs examination of an inbound shipment, a state enforcement action, a complaint from a competitor or consumer, or a lawsuit after an injury.

Direct sellers also carry the full recall burden alone. If a defect surfaces, you are responsible for the recall mechanics, customer notification, and remedy that a platform would otherwise partially organize. Payment processors and insurers serving e-commerce merchants may also impose their own compliance conditions for children's products. Operating without the required testing and certification on a direct storefront is not a lower-risk route; it is the same risk with fewer warnings before it lands.
