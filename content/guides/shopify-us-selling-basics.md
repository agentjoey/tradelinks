## Who this is for

This guide is for cross-border sellers who sell, or plan to sell, to customers in the United States through their own Shopify storefront rather than through a marketplace. It applies to general consumer products across all categories. It is relevant at two moments: before you open the store, when you choose a payment setup and decide how you will market it, and after launch, when you begin collecting orders, customer data, and potentially US state tax obligations.

The central fact of the Shopify model is that you are the merchant of record on your own site. There is no marketplace sitting between you and the customer to absorb compliance duties, collect tax on your behalf, or screen your claims before they go live. The obligations described here attach to you directly.

## What changes the decision

Whether a specific rule in this guide applies to you, and how heavily, depends on a small set of factors.

- **Your business location and payment eligibility.** Shopify Payments is available only for businesses based in supported countries and operating permitted business types. If you are not eligible, you will use a third-party payment gateway, which changes your fees, your chargeback handling, and which party holds your card-data compliance burden.
- **Your footprint in US states.** Sales-tax duties depend on where you have physical presence (inventory, staff, offices) and where your sales volume crosses state-specific economic nexus thresholds. A low-volume seller with no US footprint may have no collection duty yet; that can change quickly as volume grows.
- **How you market.** If you use endorsements, testimonials, influencer posts, or incentivized reviews anywhere that drives traffic to your store, the FTC Endorsement Guides apply to that content. If you only use plain product descriptions, the endorsement layer drops away, but the general prohibition on deceptive claims remains.
- **What customer data you collect.** Email addresses, purchase histories, and behavioral tracking through ad pixels all affect which privacy obligations and state privacy laws may reach you. A store that collects email for a newsletter faces fewer questions than one running aggressive retargeting.
- **What you sell.** Certain product categories are restricted or prohibited under Shopify's Acceptable Use Policy and under card-network rules regardless of their legality. Product-specific federal labeling and safety regimes are outside this guide's scope, but category choice still matters at the platform and payment layers.
- **Fulfillment path.** Where your inventory sits affects both state tax nexus and the shipping representations you can honestly make at checkout.

## US requirements

**Deceptive practices baseline.** Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act (15 U.S.C. 45) prohibits unfair or deceptive acts or practices in commerce. Because you write your own product pages, pricing displays, and promotional copy, this standard lands on you directly. Claims about what a product does, about discounts relative to a former price, about scarcity, and about shipping speed must be truthful and substantiated. The FTC can and does act against individual online merchants, not only large platforms.

**Endorsements and testimonials.** The FTC Endorsement Guides, codified at 16 CFR Part 255, require that any material connection between an endorser and your business be clearly and conspicuously disclosed. A material connection includes payment, free products, discounts, affiliate commissions, or an employment or family relationship. This covers influencer posts that link to your store, reviews you solicited in exchange for a free item, and testimonials you republish on your own product pages. Editing or curating reviews so that negative ones disappear can itself be deceptive. If you run an affiliate program, your affiliates' disclosure failures can be attributed to you.

**Shipping and order fulfillment.** The FTC's Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule, 16 CFR Part 435, applies to orders placed online. In general terms, it requires that you have a reasonable basis for any shipping timeframe you state, that you ship within the stated timeframe, and that when you cannot, you notify the customer and obtain consent to the delay or issue a prompt refund. If you state no timeframe, a default expectation applies under the rule; confirm the specifics at the source before relying on them. For a cross-border seller with longer delivery windows, this rule makes the shipping promise shown at checkout a load-bearing representation.

**Email marketing.** If you send commercial email to US customers, the CAN-SPAM Act of 2003 sets baseline requirements: accurate header information, non-deceptive subject lines, identification of the message as an advertisement where applicable, a valid physical postal address, and a working opt-out mechanism that you honor. Individual state laws and your email service provider's terms add further constraints.

**Sales tax, at a general level.** There is no federal sales tax in the United States. Sales tax is imposed at the state level, and below that by local jurisdictions. Following the US Supreme Court's 2018 decision in South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc., states may require out-of-state sellers to collect and remit sales tax once the seller's activity in the state crosses an economic nexus threshold, without any physical presence in the state. Thresholds vary by state; many states adopted a revenue-based threshold of `[UNVERIFIED — confirm at source]` per year, and some also apply or historically applied a transaction-count test. You must confirm the current threshold and registration process for each state where you may have nexus; do not rely on a figure from any secondary summary. Marketplace facilitator laws, which shift collection duty to marketplaces such as Amazon, generally do not cover sales you make through your own storefront. On Shopify, you are responsible for determining where you have nexus, registering with the state tax authority, configuring collection, and filing returns.

**Privacy.** The United States has no single comprehensive federal consumer privacy law. The FTC uses Section 5 against deceptive privacy and data-security practices, which means your privacy policy must match what your store actually does; a policy you do not follow is worse than a plain one you do. In addition, several states have enacted comprehensive consumer privacy statutes, of which the California Consumer Privacy Act is the best known. These laws typically apply only above certain business-size or data-volume thresholds, and the thresholds differ by state; whether any given statute applies to your store is a question to verify at `[UNVERIFIED — confirm at source]`. As a practical baseline: publish a privacy policy that accurately describes what you collect, why, and who you share it with (including advertising pixels and analytics), honor the opt-outs your tools generate, and secure the data you hold.

**Payments and card-network rules.** When you accept cards, you operate inside rules set by the card networks (for example Visa and Mastercard) and enforced through your payment processor. These cover prohibited and restricted business categories, chargeback management, refund disclosure, and data security. The card-industry security baseline is the PCI Data Security Standard, maintained by the PCI Security Standards Council. When you use Shopify Payments or another hosted checkout, the processor handles most cardholder-data exposure, but merchant-level obligations do not disappear entirely; confirm your scope with your processor.

## Amazon US

This guide's platform coverage is Shopify only. This section exists because many sellers operate on both channels, and the contrast matters. On Amazon US, the marketplace layer absorbs several of the duties described above: Amazon processes the payment, marketplace facilitator laws generally make Amazon the party that collects and remits state sales tax on Amazon sales, and Amazon enforces its own listing, review, and advertising policies, including documentation requests and category gating, at the account and listing level. None of that coverage follows you to your own storefront. A seller who is fully compliant on Amazon has, by default, done almost none of the payments, tax, or disclosure work required on a Shopify store. Treat the two channels as separate compliance surfaces.

## Shopify US

On Shopify there is no gatekeeper reviewing your product pages, vetting your claims, or collecting tax on your behalf before payout. The duties described in the federal section attach to you directly, and Shopify's role is contractual and operational rather than supervisory in the marketplace sense.

**Shopify Payments eligibility.** Shopify Payments is available only in supported countries and for permitted business types, and onboarding involves identity and business verification. Eligibility criteria, supported country lists, and any payout timing or reserve practices change over time; confirm the current terms at `[UNVERIFIED — confirm at source]` before building a plan around them. If you are not eligible, you will route payments through a third-party gateway, which introduces its own underwriting, fees, and compliance questionnaire.

**Acceptable use.** Shopify's Acceptable Use Policy prohibits categories of goods and conduct on the platform independent of their legality, and card-network rules impose a further restricted-business layer underneath. Violations can result in payment holds or store termination without the structured appeal process a marketplace might offer.

**Tax settings are tools, not compliance.** Shopify provides tax calculation and collection settings, and can register reminders and reports, but the legal duty to determine nexus, register, collect correctly, and remit remains yours. Turning on a tax setting in the admin is not the same as being registered with the state.

**You hold the customer relationship.** Refund policies, privacy disclosures, shipping promises, and marketing consent on your storefront are yours to set and yours to answer for. Card-network chargeback rules also reach you directly through your processor, and sustained chargeback or dispute problems can threaten your ability to process cards at all.
