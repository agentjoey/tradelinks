## Who this is for

This guide is for sellers based outside the United States who are considering selling goods to US customers, whether through a marketplace such as Amazon US or through their own storefront on a platform such as Shopify. It applies regardless of product category. If you manufacture or source goods abroad and ship them to US buyers, or hold inventory in US fulfillment centers, this guide describes the baseline obligations you should understand before listing anything.

It is orientation, not a checklist for a specific product. Category-specific rules — for food, cosmetics, electronics, children's products, batteries, and similar regulated goods — sit on top of everything described here and are covered in separate guides. If you already know your product falls into a regulated category, read this guide first and then the category-specific material.

## What changes the decision

Several factors move a seller between "this baseline is all I need" and "additional layers apply":

- **Product composition and function.** What the product is made of, what it does, and how it is powered determine which federal agencies have jurisdiction. A plain textile item and a radio-frequency device are treated very differently at the border and afterward.
- **Claims made.** The marketing claims attached to a product can reclassify it legally. A skin cream sold as moisturizing is a cosmetic; the same cream marketed as treating a condition may be regulated as a drug. Claims made in your listing copy, packaging, and advertising all count.
- **Battery or wireless content.** Products containing lithium batteries or radio transmitters attract additional transport, safety, and equipment-authorization rules.
- **Children's use.** Products intended for children are subject to a distinct and stricter federal regime.
- **Fulfillment path.** Whether you ship each order directly to the customer (cross-border fulfillment) or import inventory in bulk into a US warehouse or Amazon fulfillment center changes who acts as importer, what entry type is used, and when duties are paid.
- **Business footprint.** Whether you have US-based inventory, employees, or offices affects both business-registration and tax obligations.

If none of the special factors apply, the baseline in the next section still applies in full.

## US requirements

### The importer of record

Every commercial importation into the United States has an **importer of record** — the party legally responsible to U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) for the entry. Under the framework described in CBP's publication "Importing into the United States: A Guide for Commercial Importers," the importer of record must exercise **reasonable care** in classifying the goods, declaring their value, and ensuring the entry complies with US law. This responsibility cannot be outsourced by contract: a customs broker can file on your behalf, but the legal duty remains with the importer of record.

For a cross-border seller, the practical question is who that importer is. If you import inventory in bulk into a US warehouse, you (or your US entity) will normally be the importer of record. If you ship individual orders direct to consumers, the answer depends on the shipping arrangement and terms of sale, and must be established deliberately rather than assumed. Misunderstanding this point is one of the most common structural errors new sellers make.

### Customs entry and HTS classification

Customs entries are governed by Title 19 of the Code of Federal Regulations. The entry process — what documentation is filed, when, and by whom — is set out in 19 CFR Part 141. The underlying statute is the Tariff Act of 1930, whose entry provisions are codified at 19 U.S.C. 1484.

Classification means assigning each product a code in the **Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States (HTSUS)**, published by the US International Trade Commission. The classification determines the duty rate and whether additional requirements (quotas, partner-agency rules) attach. Classification is the importer's responsibility, and errors compound: a wrong code can mean underpaid duties, penalties, and retroactive bills going back years. If you are not confident in a classification, a customs broker or a binding ruling from CBP is the standard remedy.

Imported goods must also be marked with their country of origin. The marking statute is 19 U.S.C. 1304, and its implementing regulations are at 19 CFR Part 134. The general rule is that the ultimate purchaser in the United States must be able to see where the product was made; marking requirements reach the product and often its packaging.

### The federal layer below customs

Customs compliance gets goods across the border. It does not make them legal to sell. Depending on the product, other federal regimes apply before the first sale — for example, equipment authorization for radio-frequency devices, safety standards for consumer products, or labeling rules for food, drugs, and cosmetics. These are category-specific and outside this guide's scope, but the structural point is general: **admissibility at the border and legality of sale are separate questions, answered by different agencies under different statutes.**

### Federal, state, and local layering

US regulation is layered. Federal law sets a floor in many areas (product safety, customs, advertising), but states and sometimes cities and counties impose their own rules on top. Nothing about federal compliance preempts state obligations in general. A seller must expect obligations at more than one level, including state consumer-protection laws, state labeling or chemical-disclosure laws in some categories, and local business-licensing rules. This guide covers only the federal layer and general concepts; specific state regimes are out of scope.

### Business registration and tax nexus

To operate in the US market you will generally need to decide on a business structure. Foreign sellers commonly either sell through their existing foreign entity or form a US entity; each path has registration, banking, and tax consequences that depend on your circumstances. Obtaining a US Employer Identification Number (EIN) from the Internal Revenue Service is a common early step for customs and banking purposes.

On tax, two concepts matter at a general level:

- **Income tax.** Whether your sales create a US federal income tax obligation depends on your structure, activities, and any applicable tax treaty. This is fact-specific; take advice rather than assume.
- **State sales tax.** Following the Supreme Court's decision in *South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc.* (2018), states may require out-of-state sellers — including foreign sellers — to collect sales tax once they have sufficient economic connection ("economic nexus") with the state. Each state sets its own thresholds and rules; the specific dollar or transaction thresholds are `[UNVERIFIED — confirm at source]` per state. Inventory stored in a state (including in an Amazon fulfillment center) can create a physical presence that triggers obligations independent of economic thresholds.

### What "compliance" means before any sale

Before the first US sale, a compliant seller can answer, with documentation: who is the importer of record; what the correct HTS classification and duty treatment is; that country-of-origin marking is in place; which federal agencies regulate the product and that their requirements are met; which states the seller has tax obligations in; and what records will be kept to prove all of this. If any answer is missing, the sale should wait.

## Amazon US

Amazon's rules sit **on top of** the law; they never replace it. Complying with Amazon policy does not make a product legally compliant, and legal compliance does not guarantee Amazon will list a product.

In general, verifiable terms, Amazon US operates the following concepts relevant to a new cross-border seller:

- **Identity and business verification.** Sellers must register a selling account and pass identity verification; business information must be accurate and kept current.
- **Category and product gating.** Some categories and products require approval before listing, and approval may require invoices, certificates, or test reports. The specific documents vary by category and change over time; check Seller Central for the current requirements for your product.
- **Compliance documentation requests.** Amazon may request safety or compliance documentation for a listed product at any time, and may suppress or remove listings when documentation is missing or unsatisfactory.
- **Policy enforcement.** Amazon enforces its own policies (including restricted-products rules) independently of regulators, through listing suppression, account suspension, or fund holds.

Treat Amazon's requests as a separate track running in parallel with your legal obligations, not as a substitute for them.

## Shopify US

With Shopify, you operate your own storefront. Shopify provides the software and, if you use it, payment processing; it is not a marketplace gatekeeper reviewing your products before they go live. The practical consequences:

- **No pre-listing gate.** Nothing stands between you and a non-compliant listing except your own controls. The legal duties described above land directly and entirely on you.
- **Payments and taxes.** You are responsible for configuring taxes, including state sales-tax collection where you have nexus; the platform provides tooling but does not determine your obligations.
- **Platform terms still apply.** Shopify's terms of service prohibit unlawful products and conduct, and payment processors impose their own acceptable-use rules. Violations can lead to store or payment suspension, but enforcement generally follows complaints or reviews rather than pre-approval.
- **You hold the customer relationship.** Consumer-facing duties — disclosures, refunds under your stated policies, advertising truthfulness, data handling — are yours to implement and document.

The absence of a gatekeeper is a speed advantage and a compliance risk at the same time.
