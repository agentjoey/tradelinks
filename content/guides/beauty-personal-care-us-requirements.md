## Who this is for

This guide is for cross-border sellers — manufacturers, brand owners, importers, and resellers based outside the United States — who sell beauty and personal care products into the US market through Amazon US or a Shopify storefront. It applies to products such as skin care, hair care, makeup, fragrance, soaps, and similar topical preparations offered to US consumers.

It is orientation, not a compliance manual. Its purpose is to help you identify which US regulatory concepts attach to your product before you list it, so you know what to verify against the primary sources. If your product is a topical cosmetic or makes claims that touch on treating a condition, the requirements described here are very likely relevant to you.

## What changes the decision

Several factors determine which rules apply and how heavily:

- **Product composition and intended use.** A moisturizer that hydrates skin is generally a cosmetic. A product intended to treat acne, protect against sunburn, or control dandruff is regulated as a drug in the US, even if it looks and is marketed like a cosmetic. The claims you make — on the label, the listing page, and in advertising — can move a product across this line.
- **Claims made.** Structure/function-style claims about appearance (cleanses, softens, beautifies) stay in cosmetic territory. Claims to treat, cure, mitigate, or prevent disease, or to affect the structure or function of the body, place the product in drug territory. This is the single most common way cross-border sellers create unexpected regulatory exposure.
- **Color additives.** If your product contains color additives, US law requires that most of them be FDA-approved for the specific intended use, and some must come from FDA-certified batches.
- **Battery or wireless content.** Products such as LED facial devices, cleansing brushes, or hair tools with rechargeable batteries or radio functions attract additional regimes outside this guide's focus (battery transport rules, FCC equipment authorization, and in some cases medical device rules if therapeutic claims are made).
- **Children's use.** Products intended for children can attract additional consumer product safety requirements not covered here.
- **Fulfillment path.** Whether you ship direct to consumers, use Amazon FBA, or hold inventory in a US warehouse does not change the underlying federal product rules, but it changes who holds records, who receives FDA or platform inquiries, and how a recall would reach your customers.
- **Your role.** The "responsible person" concept under the Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act of 2022 (MoCRA) attaches specific duties to the entity named on the label. If that is your company, the obligations are yours even if a contract manufacturer made the product.

## US requirements

Cosmetics in the US are regulated by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act). Unlike drugs, most cosmetics do not require premarket approval before sale, but they must not be adulterated or misbranded, and the burden of compliance sits on the seller and the responsible person.

**MoCRA.** The Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act of 2022 substantially expanded FDA's authority over cosmetics. Its core concepts include:

- **Facility registration.** Facilities that manufacture or process cosmetic products for US distribution must register with FDA. Foreign facilities are included.
- **Product listing.** Each cosmetic product marketed in the US must be listed with FDA by the responsible person, including ingredient information.
- **Adverse-event records.** The responsible person must maintain records of adverse events associated with the product and report serious adverse events to FDA.
- **Safety substantiation.** The responsible person must ensure and maintain records supporting adequate substantiation of the product's safety.
- **Good manufacturing practice and fragrance allergen labeling.** MoCRA directs FDA to establish or address these; the status of specific rulemakings and their compliance dates should be confirmed at source.

Do not rely on any date or threshold from a secondary article without checking it against FDA's own materials.

**Labeling.** Cosmetic labeling is governed by the FD&C Act and the Fair Packaging and Labeling Act, with detailed rules in **21 CFR Part 701**. Key concepts:

- **Identity statement, net quantity of contents, and name and place of business** of the manufacturer, packer, or distributor on the label.
- **Ingredient declaration.** Ingredients must be listed in descending order of predominance, using established names, subject to the rules in 21 CFR Part 701. There are special treatments for color additives, incidental ingredients, and trade-secret ingredients.
- **Warnings.** Certain products require specific warning statements (for example, aerosol products or products whose safety has not been substantiated).
- Labels must be truthful and not misleading; misbranded products are subject to enforcement.

**The cosmetic/drug line.** Under the FD&C Act, a product is a drug if it is intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent disease, or to affect the structure or any function of the body. In personal care, several familiar categories are regulated as over-the-counter (OTC) drugs in the US: **sunscreens**, **anti-acne products**, and **anti-dandruff shampoos** are standard examples. These products are subject to OTC drug requirements (historically monographs, now under a reformed OTC framework) covering permitted active ingredients, concentrations, and labeling — a materially heavier regime than cosmetics. A single product can be both a cosmetic and a drug (an anti-dandruff shampoo that also cleans hair). Sellers should review every claim on their packaging, listing copy, bullet points, and A+ content with this boundary in mind.

**Color additives.** The FD&C Act requires that color additives used in cosmetics be approved by FDA for their intended use, and certain color additives (generally those derived from coal-tar/petroleum sources) must be batch-certified by FDA. Using an unapproved color additive, or an approved one for an unapproved use (around the eye is a common trap), adulterates the product.

**Import and recall mechanics.** Imported cosmetics are subject to FDA examination at entry and may be detained if they appear adulterated or misbranded. Under MoCRA, FDA also has mandatory recall authority for cosmetics in defined circumstances. Sellers should be able to trace inventory and customer orders sufficiently to execute a recall; this is a PRODUCT_SAFETY_RECALLS topic that deserves a written procedure, not improvisation.

## Amazon US

Amazon operates as a gatekeeper layered on top of the federal rules. In general, verifiable terms:

- Amazon treats beauty and personal care as a category in which it may require approval to sell, and it may request documentation such as product images, ingredient lists, invoices, or safety and compliance documents at listing time or later.
- Amazon runs compliance review processes that can suppress or remove listings it believes violate its policies or applicable law, and it can request corrective documentation within stated windows.
- Amazon's policies restrict prohibited products and claims; listings making disease-treatment claims for products not authorized as drugs are a known enforcement target.
- Fulfillment by Amazon does not transfer regulatory responsibility to Amazon. The seller remains responsible for the legality of the product and its labeling.

Specific documentation lists, category gating criteria, and review timelines vary over time and by product type; confirm current requirements inside Seller Central for your specific product before listing. Nothing here should be read as a rule about any particular ASIN.

## Shopify US

On your own Shopify storefront there is no platform gatekeeper screening your listings before they go live. That changes the risk profile rather than reducing it:

- The full weight of the FD&C Act, MoCRA, the Fair Packaging and Labeling Act, and 21 CFR Part 701 lands directly on you. Nobody reviews your claims before publication, so an over-claimed product page can ship to customers for months before a warning letter, a competitor complaint, or a consumer report surfaces it.
- Your storefront copy, product descriptions, and marketing emails are all labeling-adjacent claims in regulatory terms. The cosmetic/drug line applies to your own site exactly as it applies on Amazon.
- Payment processors, shipping carriers, and advertising platforms impose their own acceptable-product policies, which can suspend your ability to transact independently of FDA.
- You are the recall execution path. Maintain customer contact records and order histories adequate to notify purchasers if a safety issue emerges.
